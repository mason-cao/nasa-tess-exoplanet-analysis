Abstract

TOI-3505.01 is a planet candidate found by NASA's TESS mission. It circles a 6220 K star that is 1.34 times the radius of the Sun (TIC 390988385, V = 11.2) every 2.9151 days. On July 22, 2022 we watched the star with the 0.8 m telescope at George Mason University during the hours the transit was supposed to happen. We took 283 images in R band and kept 263 good measurements, with a scatter of 2.50 ppt. The star never dimmed (−0.66 ± 0.39 ppt). To see whether that meant anything, we added fake transits to our data. A fake 2.91 ppt transit came back at 5.7σ, so we would have caught a real one.

We saw nothing because the prediction was off. With the period we have now, the closest transit was 17.4 hours before we started. So we went to the TESS archive and timed 25 transits in Sectors 14, 41, 54, and 81, covering 5.05 years. Fitting a line through those times gives a period of 2.9151503 ± 0.0000057 days, twice as precise as the TESS catalog value and matching it to within 0.4σ. It also tightens the predicted transit time for 2026 from 9 minutes to 3.

Materials and Methods

Taking and measuring the images

  • Mason 0.8 m telescope, July 22, 2022 UT. R filter, 50 second exposures, 0.362″ per pixel. We got 283 images and 281 of them lined up into one 5.07 hour run.
  • We subtracted darks, divided by flats, and shifted every image so the stars matched, using 25 reference stars, then redid each step in Python to check it. Astrometry.net matched our field to sky coordinates and landed 0.47 px from our measured position.
  • We measured brightness in AstroImageJ with a 25 px (9.1″) aperture, a 70–139 px sky ring, and comparison stars C2–C11. We picked 25 px by trying every radius from 20 to 50 px and keeping the one with the least scatter. Our own Python photometry agrees with AstroImageJ at r = 0.990.
  • We cut 18 measurements for bad image quality and wrote down why for each, leaving 263. No detrending passed the test we set up ahead of time, so the light curve is left as measured.

Working with the TESS archive

  • We pulled QLP light curves for Sectors 14, 41, 54, and 81, plus the SPOC light curves and the official Data Validation reports.
  • We ran a box least-squares search on each sector by itself, then fit each transit one at a time to get 25 mid-transit times and fit a straight line through them.
  • We used the TESS pixel data to build difference images, find where on the sky the dimming happens, and check how much light nearby stars add. We locked in our cuts before looking at the final numbers.

Discussion

Why we saw no transit

We picked this night using a 2022 prediction. With the period we have now, the nearest transit was 17.4 hours before we started, so there was nothing to catch. Our run also lands in a gap in TESS Sector 54, so we cannot line the two up. We added fake 2.91 ppt transits to our data and pulled them back out at 5.7σ, so a real transit would have shown up. Our flat light curve is a real limit and not just noise.

What the TESS archive gave us

One night could not fix the prediction, so we used the archive instead. Timing 25 transits across four sectors gives P = 2.9151503 ± 0.0000057 d. That is 2.05 times more precise than the catalog and agrees with it to 0.4σ. The timing points scatter more than their error bars allow (χ²/dof = 5.3, RMS 11.8 min). That is what we expect from Sector 14, where each image covers 30 minutes, so we widened the error bars instead of claiming the tighter number. Leaving Sector 14 out shifts the period by much less than 1σ.

Why the planet's size is uncertain

In the official SPOC fit the planet barely clips the edge of the star (b = 0.92). For that fit to work, the star would have to be 3.6 times less dense than a 1.34 R☉, 1.25 M☉ star can be. When a transit is this close to the edge, the depth, the length, b, and a/R★ all trade off, so lots of combinations fit the same data. If we keep the star's density and match our own 2.06 h transit length, we get b = 0.85 and a/R★ = 6.93. Either way the planet only grazes the star, so 9.0 R⊕ is much less certain than its error bar makes it look.

What we cannot claim

Another star sits 0.517″ away and adds about 21 percent of the primary's light. Our telescope cannot separate the two and neither can TESS, so if the transit belongs to the brighter star, the real depth and radius are larger. Our SPOC comparisons use the same TESS data we used, so they test our method rather than give us a second detection. We improved the orbit and ruled out several false alarms, but we did not confirm a planet.

Conclusions

  • A better period. Timing 25 transits over 5.05 years gives P = 2.9151503 ± 0.0000057 d, twice as precise as the catalog value.
  • Better predictions. The transit time for 2026 is now good to 3 minutes instead of 9, so the next person to observe needs much less time on either side of it.
  • Our night still counts. The 2.50 ppt light curve rules out a transit during the window we watched, and our fake-transit tests show we would have seen one.
  • Still a good candidate. Nothing we checked points to a false alarm. The signal looks like a roughly Jupiter-sized planet grazing the edge of its star.
  • What holds us back. The grazing path and the nearby star limit the size measurement now, not our photometry.

Future Work

  • Watch a transit using our new period. A 3 minute window makes that worth doing on a 0.8 m telescope.
  • Fit a real limb-darkened model with MCMC and a density prior to pin down b and the planet's size.
  • Observe in more than one filter, since a depth that changes with color would mean a blended eclipsing binary. Measure how bright the nearby star is in the TESS band, and take radial velocities to weigh the planet.
