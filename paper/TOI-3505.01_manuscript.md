<div class="abstract">
<p><span class="abstract-label">Context.</span> Transit ephemerides lose predictive precision when their period or epoch is superseded, which can move a short ground-based observing sequence away from the event it was intended to cover. TOI-3505.01 (TIC 390988385) remains listed as a planet candidate.</p>

<p><span class="abstract-label">Aims.</span> We reanalyzed a 2022 George Mason University (GMU) 0.8 m R-band sequence, combined the four public TESS sectors, tested the stability of an updated linear ephemeris, and investigated why the ground sequence did not show the cataloged signal.</p>

<p><span class="abstract-label">Methods.</span> We performed differential aperture photometry on {{GROUND_USED}} quality-reviewed ground measurements and measured individual TESS events with a local linear baseline and an exposure-integrated trapezoid. The primary timing fit used one light curve per event: QLP for Sectors 14 and 41 and two-minute SPOC photometry for Sectors 54 and 81. We tested sector aggregation, event and sector deletion, selection thresholds, a quadratic timing model, and one public MuSCAT2 timing as an external control. We also propagated public 2021 timing markers with the superseded period recorded in a later ExoFOP report.</p>

<p><span class="abstract-label">Results.</span> The fixed 2022 schedule window gives a depth of {{GROUND_DEPTH}} ± {{GROUND_DEPTH_ERROR}} ppt and therefore no transit-like dimming. The adopted ephemeris uses {{EVENTS}} TESS mid-transit times over {{BASELINE_YEARS}} yr and gives <em>P</em> = {{PERIOD_SHORT}} ± {{PERIOD_ERROR_SHORT}} d and <em>T</em><sub>0</sub> = {{EPOCH}} ± {{EPOCH_ERROR}} BJD<sub>TDB</sub>. A quadratic term is not favored (ΔBIC = {{DELTA_BIC}}). The current ephemeris places the relevant event {{CURRENT_OFFSET_HOURS}} h before the 2022 schedule midpoint. In contrast, propagating the public 2021 ingress, midpoint, and egress by {{RECONSTRUCTION_CYCLES}} cycles at the superseded {{OLD_PERIOD}} d period reproduces all three schedule markers within {{MAX_MARKER_OFFSET}} s.</p>

<p><span class="abstract-label">Conclusions.</span> The 2022 null observation is consistent with a stale schedule window rather than evidence against the recurring TESS signal. The updated timing solution is robust to the tested controls, but the unresolved {{COMPANION_SEPARATION}} arcsec companion and uncertain dilution prevent a physical radius measurement. This work does not validate or confirm TOI-3505.01.</p>

## 1. Introduction

The Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS) searches bright stars for periodic decreases in flux produced when an orbiting body crosses the stellar disk (Ricker et al. 2015). TESS Objects of Interest (TOIs) are candidates that require additional scrutiny because an aperture-level transit signal can also be produced by stellar variability, instrumental systematics, or an eclipsing source blended with the target. Ground-based time-series photometry can test whether an event occurs on the expected star, at the expected time, and with an approximately compatible depth and duration. Its interpretation depends on an ephemeris that is current enough to place the event inside the observing window.

TOI-3505.01 is associated with TIC 390988385 at α = {{TARGET_RA_HMS}}, δ = {{TARGET_DEC_DMS}} (ICRS; {{TARGET_RA_DEG}}°, {{TARGET_DEC_DEG}}°). The TIC v8 snapshot gives <em>T</em> = {{TARGET_TMAG}}, an effective temperature of {{TARGET_TEFF}} K, and a stellar radius estimate of {{TARGET_RADIUS}} R<sub>☉</sub> (Stassun et al. 2019). The TOI catalog quantities frozen for this analysis are <em>P</em> = {{CATALOG_PERIOD}} ± {{CATALOG_PERIOD_ERROR}} d, epoch {{CATALOG_EPOCH}} BJD<sub>TDB</sub>, depth {{CATALOG_DEPTH}} ppt, and duration {{CATALOG_DURATION}} h. A close companion reported in the project context is separated by {{COMPANION_SEPARATION}} arcsec with Δ<em>I</em> = {{COMPANION_DELTA_I}} mag. That separation is far below a TESS pixel and below the effective resolution of the ground apertures used here.

The GMU program obtained an R-band sequence during the night of UT 2022 July 22. The recovered scheduling row placed ingress and egress inside that sequence, yet the reduced light curve did not show a transit. A null observation can have several explanations: inadequate precision, an astrophysical false positive, an event outside the data, or a stale prediction. The public record later acquired additional TESS sectors and ground reports, creating enough timing leverage to distinguish among those possibilities without treating any report-level result as an independent validation.

In this paper, we combine all four public TESS sectors available for TOI-3505.01, construct a {{EVENTS}}-event timing solution, quantify its sensitivity to analysis choices, and reconstruct the historical 2022 schedule. {{NOVELTY_SENTENCE}} This deliberately narrow novelty statement does not claim the first detection, classification, follow-up observation, validation, or confirmation of the candidate.

Section 2 inventories the target, TESS products, GMU sequence, and public follow-up context. Section 3 describes the ground and TESS analyses, timing controls, schedule reconstruction, and localization screens. Section 4 reports the measurements. Section 5 interprets the null observation and defines the limits of the candidate-level result. Section 6 summarizes the conclusions and future work.

## 3. Analysis

### 3.1. Ground reduction and differential photometry

We reduced each science image as (science − median 50 s dark) / median normalized R flat. No separate bias subtraction, dark-time scaling, cosmic-ray filter, or interpolating registration was applied. We registered {{GROUND_ARCHIVED}} frames to the first image using integer-pixel shifts derived from 25 reference stars. The median post-registration stellar-position RMS was 0.526 pixel.

We used AstroImageJ-style differential aperture photometry (Collins et al. 2017) with a 25 pixel source radius, a 70–139 pixel sky annulus, and the summed counts of ten fixed comparison stars, C2–C11. The final curve uses {{GROUND_USED}} measurements and excludes {{GROUND_EXCLUDED}} measurements for predeclared condition failures or a field-wide image artifact. Target faintness by itself was never an exclusion criterion. The adopted relative flux was not detrended: none of the tested airmass, sky, width, position, comparison-count, centroid, or combined models passed the blocked cross-validation promotion rules.

We measured the ground precision with the robust scatter of the unbinned adopted flux. For the historical-window test, we fit a straight local baseline plus a fixed, exposure-integrated box between the schedule ingress and egress. The event time and duration were not optimized. We then injected a {{INJECTION_DEPTH}} ppt box at the same fixed times and repeated the fit. This same-window injection tests the arithmetic and the sensitivity of this light curve at the historical window; it is not a target-only pixel simulation and does not solve dilution.

### 3.2. TESS event times and the linear ephemeris

The uniform sector analysis searched a local period and duration grid with a finite-exposure box and one straight baseline per event. For the final timing analysis, we used an exposure-integrated symmetric trapezoid. Its flat-to-total duration fraction, <em>T</em><sub>23</sub>/<em>T</em><sub>14</sub> = 0.378, was derived from the SPOC multi-sector radius ratio and impact parameter. This approximate shape represents the long ingress and egress of the near-grazing solution without claiming a limb-darkened physical fit.

Each event was measured within a ±0.20 d local window. We accepted a midpoint if the local window was complete, its sampled-window coverage was at least 0.70, the fitted depth signal-to-noise ratio was at least 2.5, and its timing uncertainty was no greater than 0.03 d. These rules retained {{EVENTS}} midpoints: four in Sector 14, eight in Sector 41, nine in Sector 54, and six in Sector 81. QLP was used in Sectors 14 and 41, and SPOC two-minute data were used in Sectors 54 and 81, so each transit entered once.

We fit the accepted midpoints with

<div class="equation"><em>T</em>(<em>E</em>) = <em>T</em><sub>0</sub> + <em>P E</em>,</div>

where <em>E</em> is the integer cycle relative to the catalog epoch. Weighted least squares used the event-level midpoint errors. When χ²<sub>ν</sub> exceeded one, we multiplied the covariance-derived parameter errors by √χ²<sub>ν</sub>; for the adopted fit the scale is {{ERROR_SCALE}}. We retained the epoch–period covariance when propagating prediction uncertainties. Times are reported in BJD<sub>TDB</sub>; conversions followed the barycentric timing conventions of Eastman et al. (2010) and used Astropy (Astropy Collaboration et al. 2022).

### 3.3. Timing robustness and model comparison

We evaluated whether the period depended on individual events, sector sampling, event-selection thresholds, or model complexity. First, we collapsed each sector to one conservative timing anchor; each anchor uncertainty was the larger of its formal weighted-mean error and its empirical within-sector standard error. Second, we refit after omitting each sector and after omitting each accepted event. Because there are only four sector clusters, the delete-one-sector jackknife is a sensitivity scale rather than a calibrated replacement for the primary uncertainty. Third, we repeated the fit at depth-S/N thresholds of 3 and 4.

We compared the linear ephemeris with a pivoted quadratic timing model,

<div class="equation"><em>T</em>(<em>E</em>) = <em>a</em> + <em>b</em>(<em>E</em> − <em>E</em><sub>p</sub>) + <em>q</em>(<em>E</em> − <em>E</em><sub>p</sub>)²,</div>

using the Bayesian information criterion (BIC; Schwarz 1978). Finally, we appended the public MuSCAT2 midpoint, BJD<sub>TDB</sub> 2460140.4350453 ± 0.000639, to a separate 28-point control. The report describes a tentative egress, so this point never enters the headline TESS-only result.

### 3.4. Reconstruction of the 2022 schedule

The schedule row visibly lists {{REVISED_PERIOD}} d but does not preserve its prediction epoch. We therefore searched the public report history for a prior event whose propagated ingress, midpoint, and egress could generate the schedule cells. A 2021 October 15 ULMT report gives all three BJD<sub>TDB</sub> markers. A later CMO report records both a superseded period of {{OLD_PERIOD}} d and the revised {{REVISED_PERIOD}} d period, and states that the revised Sector 41 ephemeris moved prior events by approximately 20 h.

We propagated each ULMT marker by the same integer number of cycles,

<div class="equation"><em>T</em><sub>2022, marker</sub> = <em>T</em><sub>2021, marker</sub> + {{RECONSTRUCTION_CYCLES}} × {{OLD_PERIOD}} d,</div>

and compared the result directly with the confirmed-EDT schedule markers. As controls, we propagated the same 2021 midpoint with the revised period and calculated the nearest event from the adopted TESS ephemeris. This is an archival consistency reconstruction. Without the original workbook, agreement cannot distinguish a stale formula from manually copied timing cells.

### 3.5. Localization and contamination screens

We compared circular TESScut apertures from 1.5 to 4.0 pixels and constructed difference images at a fixed 3.0 pixel reference radius. In every sector, the difference centroid lies within one TESS pixel of the target coordinates. This localizes the signal to the target system at TESS resolution but cannot distinguish the primary star from the {{COMPANION_SEPARATION}} arcsec companion.

For the ground images, we measured all {{NEARBY_BRIGHT}} TIC sources within 2.5 arcmin that were bright enough to mimic a {{CATALOG_DEPTH}} ppt event under a total-eclipse screen. At the confirmed schedule window, {{NEARBY_CLEARED}} sources were inconsistent with the required dimming at the screen's three-sigma criterion, while {{NEARBY_OVERLAP}} source apertures overlap the target aperture. This is a conditional project screen, not the formal program AstroImageJ nearby-eclipsing-binary procedure. Neither it nor the TESS difference images resolve the close companion.

We also calculated dilution scenarios using the TIC contamination ratio and Δ<em>I</em> as a rough TESS-band proxy. Because the QLP crowding treatment and the companion's actual TESS-band contrast are not resolved, we did not apply those scenarios as a final correction and do not report a planet radius.

## 4. Results

### 4.1. Ground light curve

The {{GROUND_USED}} accepted ground measurements have an unbinned robust scatter of {{GROUND_SCATTER}} ppt. The fixed historical-window fit gives {{GROUND_DEPTH}} ± {{GROUND_DEPTH_ERROR}} ppt, or {{GROUND_DEPTH_SNR}}σ when positive depth denotes dimming. The negative value is therefore not a transit-like event. The adopted current-catalog prediction was already {{CURRENT_GROUND_OFFSET_HOURS}} h before the first accepted ground exposure, so the ground sequence did not observe the current predicted event.

Adding a {{INJECTION_DEPTH}} ppt same-window box recovered an incremental depth of {{INJECTION_RECOVERED}} ppt. Including the measured negative control at that location, the total recovered dimming has signal-to-noise {{INJECTION_TOTAL_SNR}}. The injection demonstrates sensitivity to a catalog-scale dimming at the fixed historical window; it does not establish sensitivity to every transit shape or blend scenario.

### 4.2. Four-sector signal and adopted ephemeris

The uniform QLP analysis recovers a recurring dip in all four sectors with observed depths from 2.68 to 3.73 ppt (Figure 1). These values are consistent at the level expected for different cadences and apertures, but they are not physical, dilution-corrected depths. The official SPOC reports for Sectors 54 and 81 recover the same signal and do not show a strong odd/even, secondary-event, or centroid alert. Because they reuse the same TESS pixels, those reports are diagnostic comparisons rather than independent evidence.

The adopted linear fit is

<div class="equation"><em>T</em><sub>0</sub> = {{EPOCH}} ± {{EPOCH_ERROR}} BJD<sub>TDB</sub>, &nbsp;&nbsp; <em>P</em> = {{PERIOD}} ± {{PERIOD_ERROR}} d.</div>

It has χ²<sub>ν</sub> = {{REDUCED_CHI2}} after the stated uncertainty scaling and an RMS timing residual of {{TIMING_RMS}} min. Its formal period uncertainty is {{GAIN_CATALOG}} times smaller than the frozen TOI catalog uncertainty and {{GAIN_SPOC}} times smaller than the SPOC multi-sector value. Precision comparisons do not make the fit independent of those source observations.

### 4.3. Robustness controls

The four conservative sector anchors give <em>P</em> = {{ANCHOR_PERIOD}} ± {{ANCHOR_ERROR}} d, consistent with the primary result. The delete-one-sector jackknife scale is {{SECTOR_JACKKNIFE_ERROR}} d, or {{SECTOR_JACKKNIFE_RATIO}} times the primary formal error. We do not substitute this four-cluster value for the event-level uncertainty. The delete-one-event jackknife scale is {{EVENT_JACKKNIFE_ERROR}} d, and no single-event deletion shifts the period by more than {{EVENT_JACKKNIFE_MAX_SHIFT}} d. Raising the minimum depth S/N to 3 or 4 leaves the period within the primary formal uncertainty.

The quadratic coefficient is <em>q</em> = {{QUADRATIC_TERM}} ± {{QUADRATIC_ERROR}} d cycle<sup>−2</sup> ({{QUADRATIC_SIGMA}}σ). It improves χ² by only {{DELTA_CHI2}} while adding a parameter, yielding ΔBIC = BIC<sub>quadratic</sub> − BIC<sub>linear</sub> = {{DELTA_BIC}}. The timing data therefore do not favor curvature. Adding the tentative MuSCAT2 timing only as an external control gives {{MUSCAT_PERIOD}} ± {{MUSCAT_ERROR}} d, a {{MUSCAT_PRECISION_CHANGE}}% formal precision change, and remains consistent with the TESS-only period.

### 4.4. Historical schedule

The adopted ephemeris places the relevant midpoint {{CURRENT_OFFSET_HOURS}} h before the schedule midpoint. Propagating the public 2021 markers by {{RECONSTRUCTION_CYCLES}} cycles with the superseded {{OLD_PERIOD}} d period instead reproduces the 2022 schedule: the ingress, midpoint, and egress residuals have maximum absolute magnitude {{MAX_MARKER_OFFSET}} s (Table 3). Applying the visible {{REVISED_PERIOD}} d value to the same 2021 midpoint predicts an event {{REVISED_OFFSET_HOURS}} h before the schedule midpoint. The combined evidence is strongly consistent with timing cells retained from the superseded ephemeris while the displayed period cell was updated.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1. Interpretation of the 2022 null

The ground light curve alone answers a limited question: no transit-like dimming is measured inside the exact window the 2022 schedule told the observers to cover. The same-window injection shows that a catalog-scale box would have been recovered in that portion of the data. The updated TESS ephemeris then resolves the apparent conflict by placing the actual predicted event outside the sequence.

The schedule reconstruction provides a specific historical explanation. The three marker cells are jointly reproduced to better than one minute by a public 2021 prediction propagated with the superseded period. A coincidental match of ingress, midpoint, and egress is less plausible than a single midpoint coincidence because it also preserves the event duration. Nevertheless, we cannot inspect the missing workbook formulas or revision history. We therefore describe the result as a strongly supported archival reconstruction, not proof that a particular spreadsheet formula caused the error.

The null observation should not be used as evidence that the TESS signal disappeared. It is an off-transit observation under the adopted ephemeris. Conversely, explaining the schedule does not turn the ground data into a transit detection.

### 5.2. Timing precision and model scope

The long, four-sector baseline supplies most of the period leverage. Sectors 14 and 81 are separated by more than five years, while the two-minute SPOC sampling in the later sectors improves individual midpoint precision. The stability of the period under conservative sector aggregation, stricter selection, event deletion, sector deletion, QLP-only processing, and the external MuSCAT2 control argues that the headline period is not an artifact of one event or one threshold.

The formal uncertainty remains conditional on the approximate trapezoid, the event-level timing errors, and the single linear ephemeris. The four-sector jackknife is larger but is based on only four clusters. Reporting both quantities distinguishes a fit uncertainty from a coarse sensitivity scale. The positive ΔBIC for the quadratic model means that these timings do not justify an added curvature parameter; it does not prove that the physical period is exactly constant.

### 5.3. Candidate status and unresolved blends

The recurring signal, weak odd/even and secondary-event diagnostics, and TESS-scale centroid agreement are all compatible with a transit on the target system. They do not determine which component of the unresolved system is dimming. A {{COMPANION_SEPARATION}} arcsec companion is inseparable in the TESS images and in the 25 pixel ground aperture. The conditional nearby-star screen also leaves {{NEARBY_OVERLAP}} apertures overlapping the target and is not a formal nearby-eclipsing-binary clearance.

These limitations are why we do not apply the scenario dilution correction, infer a planet-to-star radius ratio, calculate a planet radius, or report a false-positive probability. The live public status at the analysis date remains PC. Statistical validation would require a documented combination of resolved imaging, spectroscopy or radial velocities as appropriate, fully characterized light curves, and a formal false-positive analysis beyond the scope of this work.

### 5.4. Literature and novelty boundary

TOI-3505.01 appears in the population-level Ariel target list of Edwards & Tinetti (2022), and public ground follow-up predates this analysis. Exact searches for “TOI-3505.01,” “TOI 3505,” and TIC 390988385 found no dedicated paper or preprint in the dated literature snapshot. The supported novelty is therefore the combination of all four public TESS sectors with the archival schedule reconstruction, not discovery or first follow-up. Because a search can miss an unexpected identifier or unindexed venue, the phrase “To our knowledge” must remain, and the search should be repeated immediately before submission or preprint posting.

## 6. Conclusions and future work

We analyzed the GMU 0.8 m R-band sequence and all four public TESS sectors of TOI-3505.01. The ground data show no dimming in the confirmed-EDT 2022 schedule window: {{GROUND_DEPTH}} ± {{GROUND_DEPTH_ERROR}} ppt. The primary TESS-only fit gives <em>P</em> = {{PERIOD_SHORT}} ± {{PERIOD_ERROR_SHORT}} d from {{EVENTS}} events over {{BASELINE_YEARS}} yr, and the tested resampling, selection, pipeline, external-timing, and quadratic controls do not motivate a different headline solution. The adopted event was {{CURRENT_OFFSET_HOURS}} h before the schedule midpoint, while a superseded {{OLD_PERIOD}} d public ephemeris reproduces all three schedule markers within {{MAX_MARKER_OFFSET}} s. The simplest supported interpretation is that the 2022 observations followed a stale event window.

Future work should obtain a new, full transit using the updated prediction and an independently verified observatory clock; repeat the formal nearby-eclipsing-binary procedure; measure the close companion's contrast in or near the TESS band; and acquire resolving imaging and spectroscopic constraints needed for a formal false-positive assessment. A joint, exposure-integrated, limb-darkened transit model should be attempted only after the dilution model is documented. Recovering the original 2022 workbook, formula history, Transit Info file, and clock record would further strengthen the archival interpretation. Until those steps are complete, TOI-3505.01 remains a planet candidate and no dilution-corrected radius should be quoted.
