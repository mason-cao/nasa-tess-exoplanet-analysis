<div class="abstract">
<p><span class="abstract-label">Context.</span> Transit ephemerides lose predictive precision when their period or epoch is superseded, which can move a short ground-based observing sequence away from the event it was intended to cover. TOI-3505.01 (TIC 390988385) remains listed as a planet candidate.</p>

<p><span class="abstract-label">Aims.</span> We reanalyzed a 2022 George Mason University (GMU) 0.8 m R-band sequence, combined the four public TESS sectors, tested the stability of an updated linear ephemeris, and investigated why the ground sequence did not show the cataloged signal.</p>

<p><span class="abstract-label">Methods.</span> We performed differential aperture photometry on {{GROUND_USED}} quality-reviewed ground measurements, tested the scheduled window, and scanned the whole sequence while holding the duration at a published TESS value and floating the midpoint. We injected the corresponding published depth at every admissible midpoint to evaluate phase sensitivity in the observed residuals. We measured individual TESS events with a local linear baseline and an exposure-integrated trapezoid. The primary timing fit used one light curve per event: QLP for Sectors 14 and 41 and two-minute SPOC photometry for Sectors 54 and 81. We tested sector aggregation, event and sector deletion, selection thresholds, a quadratic timing model, and one public MuSCAT2 timing as an external control. We also propagated public 2021 timing markers with the superseded period recorded in a later ExoFOP report.</p>

<p><span class="abstract-label">Results.</span> The fixed 2022 schedule window gives a depth of {{GROUND_DEPTH}} ± {{GROUND_DEPTH_ERROR}} ppt and therefore no transit-like dimming. The whole-sequence scan contains no fitted event above 3σ under the formal weighted-least-squares covariance. Exact-depth injections exceed that formal threshold at {{SEARCH_RECOVERY_CATALOG}} of admissible catalog-duration midpoints and {{SEARCH_RECOVERY_SPOC}} of SPOC-duration midpoints, so the sequence does not support exclusion at every phase. The adopted ephemeris uses {{EVENTS}} TESS mid-transit times over {{BASELINE_YEARS}} yr and gives <em>P</em> = {{PERIOD_SHORT}} ± {{PERIOD_ERROR_SHORT}} d and <em>T</em><sub>0</sub> = {{EPOCH}} ± {{EPOCH_ERROR}} BJD<sub>TDB</sub>. The delete-one-sector sensitivity scale is {{SECTOR_JACKKNIFE_ERROR}} d, and a quadratic term is not favored (ΔBIC = {{DELTA_BIC}}). The adopted ephemeris places the relevant event {{CURRENT_OFFSET_HOURS}} h before the 2022 schedule midpoint. In contrast, propagating the public 2021 ingress, midpoint, and egress by {{RECONSTRUCTION_CYCLES}} cycles at the superseded {{OLD_PERIOD}} d period reproduces all three schedule markers within {{MAX_MARKER_OFFSET}} s.</p>

<p><span class="abstract-label">Conclusions.</span> The 2022 null observation is consistent with a stale schedule window rather than evidence against the recurring TESS signal. The updated timing solution is robust to the tested controls, but the unresolved {{COMPANION_SEPARATION}} arcsec companion and uncertain dilution prevent a physical radius measurement. This work does not validate or confirm TOI-3505.01.</p>

## 1. Introduction

The Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS) searches bright stars for periodic decreases in flux produced when an orbiting body crosses the stellar disk (Ricker et al. 2015). TESS Objects of Interest (TOIs) are candidates that require additional scrutiny because an aperture-level transit signal can also be produced by stellar variability, instrumental systematics, or an eclipsing source blended with the target. Ground-based time-series photometry can test whether an event occurs on the expected star, at the expected time, and with an approximately compatible depth and duration. Its interpretation depends on an ephemeris that is current enough to place the event inside the observing window.

TOI-3505.01 is associated with TIC 390988385 at α = {{TARGET_RA_HMS}}, δ = {{TARGET_DEC_DMS}} (ICRS; {{TARGET_RA_DEG}}°, {{TARGET_DEC_DEG}}°). The TIC v8 snapshot gives <em>T</em> = {{TARGET_TMAG}}, an effective temperature of {{TARGET_TEFF}} K, and a stellar radius estimate of {{TARGET_RADIUS}} R<sub>☉</sub> (Stassun et al. 2019). The TOI catalog quantities frozen for this analysis (Guerrero et al. 2021) are <em>P</em> = {{CATALOG_PERIOD}} ± {{CATALOG_PERIOD_ERROR}} d, epoch {{CATALOG_EPOCH}} BJD<sub>TDB</sub>, depth {{CATALOG_DEPTH}} ppt, and duration {{CATALOG_DURATION}} h. A close companion reported in the project context is separated by {{COMPANION_SEPARATION}} arcsec with Δ<em>I</em> = {{COMPANION_DELTA_I}} mag. That separation is far below a TESS pixel and below the effective resolution of the ground apertures used here.

The GMU program obtained an R-band sequence during the night of UT 2022 July 22. The recovered scheduling row placed ingress and egress inside that sequence, yet the reduced light curve did not show a transit. A null observation can have several explanations: inadequate precision, an astrophysical false positive, an event outside the data, or a stale prediction. The public record later acquired additional TESS sectors and ground reports, creating enough timing leverage to distinguish among those possibilities without treating any report-level result as an independent validation.

In this paper, we combine all four public TESS sectors available for TOI-3505.01, construct a {{EVENTS}}-event timing solution, quantify its sensitivity to analysis choices, and reconstruct the historical 2022 schedule. {{NOVELTY_SENTENCE}} This deliberately narrow novelty statement does not claim the first detection, classification, follow-up observation, validation, or confirmation of the candidate.

Section 2 inventories the target, TESS products, GMU sequence, and public follow-up context. Section 3 describes the ground and TESS analyses, timing controls, schedule reconstruction, and localization screens. Section 4 reports the measurements. Section 5 interprets the null observation and defines the limits of the candidate-level result. Section 6 summarizes the conclusions and future work.

### 3.1. Ground reduction and differential photometry

We reduced each science image as (science − median 50 s dark) / median normalized R flat. No separate bias subtraction, dark-time scaling, cosmic-ray filter, or interpolating registration was applied. We registered {{GROUND_ARCHIVED}} frames to the first image using integer-pixel shifts derived from 25 reference stars. The median post-registration stellar-position RMS was 0.526 pixel.

We used AstroImageJ-style differential aperture photometry (Collins et al. 2017) with a 25 pixel source radius, a 70–139 pixel sky annulus, and the summed counts of ten fixed comparison stars, C2–C11. The final curve uses {{GROUND_USED}} measurements and excludes {{GROUND_EXCLUDED}} measurements for predeclared condition failures or a field-wide image artifact. The seeing profile, field image with apertures, plate-solved reference frame, delta-magnitude versus RMS plot, and nearby-star disposition table corresponding to TFOP SG1 files 5–7 and 9–10 are included in the repository products. Target faintness by itself was never an exclusion criterion. The adopted relative flux was not detrended: none of the tested airmass, sky, width, position, comparison-count, centroid, or combined models passed the blocked cross-validation promotion rules.

We measured the ground precision with the robust scatter of the unbinned adopted flux. For the historical-window test, we fit a straight local baseline plus a fixed, exposure-integrated box between the schedule ingress and egress. The event time and duration were not optimized. We then injected a {{INJECTION_DEPTH}} ppt box at the same fixed times and repeated the fit. This same-window injection tests the arithmetic and the sensitivity of this light curve at the historical window; it is not a target-only pixel simulation and does not solve dilution.

Because the schedule window is itself in question, we also searched the sequence independently of it. Holding the duration at a published TESS value and leaving the depth free, we scanned the event midpoint across the night in two-minute steps and refitted the same baseline-plus-box model at each trial position. We required each trial event to be at least {{SEARCH_COVERAGE}} sampled and to retain at least twenty baseline measurements on each side of it. That two-sided requirement matters: without it, trial events that hang off the end of the sequence let the baseline slope trade against depth, and the degraded final half hour of the night - where the scatter is roughly four times the mid-run value - produces a large spurious dimming. At every admissible midpoint, we then injected the corresponding published depth into the observed curve and refitted it. We report the fraction of those exact-depth injections whose total fitted depth exceeds a formal 3σ threshold. The weighted-least-squares covariance is locally scaled by reduced χ² but does not model temporal correlation, so the recovery fraction is a phase-sampling diagnostic for this residual realization, not a detection probability or ensemble completeness estimate. We quote no Gaussian false-alarm probability and make no global exclusion from the formal limits alone.

### 3.2. TESS event times and the linear ephemeris

The uniform sector analysis searched a local period and duration grid with a finite-exposure box and one straight baseline per event. For the final timing analysis, we used an exposure-integrated symmetric trapezoid. Its flat-to-total duration fraction, <em>T</em><sub>23</sub>/<em>T</em><sub>14</sub> = 0.378, was derived from the SPOC multi-sector radius ratio and impact parameter. This approximate shape represents the long ingress and egress of the near-grazing solution without claiming a limb-darkened physical fit.

Each event was measured within a ±0.20 d local window. We accepted a midpoint if the local window was complete, its sampled-window coverage was at least 0.70, the fitted depth signal-to-noise ratio was at least 2.5, and its timing uncertainty was no greater than 0.03 d. These rules retained {{EVENTS}} midpoints: four in Sector 14, eight in Sector 41, nine in Sector 54, and six in Sector 81. QLP was used in Sectors 14 and 41, and SPOC two-minute data were used in Sectors 54 and 81, so each transit entered once.

We fit the accepted midpoints with

<div class="equation"><em>T</em>(<em>E</em>) = <em>T</em><sub>0</sub> + <em>P E</em>,</div>

where <em>E</em> is the integer cycle relative to the catalog epoch. Weighted least squares used the event-level midpoint errors. When χ²<sub>ν</sub> exceeded one, we multiplied the covariance-derived parameter errors by √χ²<sub>ν</sub>; for the adopted fit the scale is {{ERROR_SCALE}}. We retained the epoch–period covariance when propagating prediction uncertainties. Times are reported in BJD<sub>TDB</sub>; conversions followed the barycentric timing conventions of Eastman et al. (2010) and used Astropy (Astropy Collaboration et al. 2022).

### 3.3. Timing robustness and model comparison

We evaluated whether the period depended on individual events, sector sampling, event-selection thresholds, or model complexity. First, we collapsed each sector to one conservative timing anchor; each anchor uncertainty was the larger of its formal weighted-mean error and its empirical within-sector standard error. Second, we refit after omitting each sector and after omitting each accepted event. Because there are only four sector clusters, the delete-one-sector jackknife is a sensitivity scale rather than a calibrated replacement for the primary uncertainty. Third, we repeated the fit at depth-S/N thresholds of 3 and 4.

We compared the linear ephemeris with a pivoted quadratic timing model,

<div class="equation"><em>T</em>(<em>E</em>) = <em>a</em> + <em>b</em>(<em>E</em> − <em>E</em><sub>p</sub>) + <em>q</em>(<em>E</em> − <em>E</em><sub>p</sub>)²,</div>

using the Bayesian information criterion (BIC; Schwarz 1978). Finally, we appended the public MuSCAT2 midpoint, BJD<sub>TDB</sub> 2460140.4350453 ± 0.000639, to a separate 28-point control. The report describes a tentative egress, so this point never enters the headline TESS-only result.

### 3.4. Reconstruction of the 2022 schedule

The schedule row visibly lists {{REVISED_PERIOD}} d but does not preserve its prediction epoch. We therefore searched the public report history for a prior event whose propagated ingress, midpoint, and egress could generate the schedule cells. We propagated each ULMT marker by the same integer number of cycles,

### 3.5. Localization and contamination screens

We compared circular TESScut apertures from 1.5 to 4.0 pixels and constructed difference images at a fixed 3.0 pixel reference radius. In every sector, the difference centroid lies within one TESS pixel of the target coordinates. This localizes the signal to the target system at TESS resolution but cannot distinguish the primary star from the {{COMPANION_SEPARATION}} arcsec companion.

For the ground images, we measured all {{NEARBY_BRIGHT}} TIC sources within 2.5 arcmin bright enough to mimic a {{CATALOG_DEPTH}} ppt event under a total-eclipse screen, applying the {{TFOP_BAND_CORRECTION}} mag correction to Δ<em>T</em> that the TFOP SG1 guidelines specify to compensate for the difference between the TESS band and the redder follow-up filter. Evaluated at the confirmed schedule window, {{NEARBY_CLEARED}} sources are cleared, {{NEARBY_OVERLAP}} are blended with the target aperture, {{NEARBY_FAINT}} are too faint to yield a decisive limit, and {{NEARBY_WEAK}} are measured but without a limit deep enough to exclude the required eclipse. Omitting the band correction changes the cleared count by two, so the screen is not sensitive to that choice.

For the {{NEARBY_WEAK}} measured but uncleared sources we asked whether the apparent dimming has the shape of an eclipse, by comparing the post-egress baseline with the pre-ingress baseline. Neither does. In both the flux fails to return to its pre-ingress level, so the apparent depth is a monotonic trend across the night rather than an event, and in both the night-long scatter exceeds the apparent depth. This is a Python reimplementation of the screen rather than the AstroImageJ macro itself, and it does not resolve the close companion; neither do the TESS difference images.

We also calculated dilution scenarios using the TIC contamination ratio and Δ<em>I</em> as a TESS-band proxy. The I band spans roughly 700–900 nm and therefore sits inside the 600–1000 nm TESS bandpass, and the Shane Δ<em>J</em> brackets it from the red side, so the companion contrast is better constrained than a generic optical-to-TESS extrapolation would be. Under the screening budget the companion contributes a flux ratio of {{COMPANION_FLUX_RATIO}} relative to the target, leaving the target {{TARGET_FLUX_FRACTION}} of the light in the aperture; if the {{CATALOG_DEPTH}} ppt catalog depth is an uncorrected observed depth, the implied host depth is {{HOST_DEPTH_SCENARIO}} ppt.

We do not adopt that scenario, for a reason worth stating explicitly: SPOC PDCSAP photometry is already corrected for catalog crowding, whereas the QLP series used in Sectors 14 and 41 is not, so Table 1 mixes corrected and uncorrected depths and a further contamination correction applied uniformly would double-count for the SPOC sectors. Resolving that, and the companion's true TESS-band contrast, is a prerequisite for a physical depth. We therefore report no dilution-corrected depth and no planet radius from this work.

### 3.6. False-positive scenario tests

The public archive supports two further tests that bear directly on the standard false-positive scenarios, and we apply both to values as published rather than re-reducing them.

First, a blended eclipsing binary generally produces a transit depth that varies with wavelength, because the blended source and the target differ in colour. The MuSCAT2 report gives simultaneous depths in <em>g</em>, <em>r</em>, <em>i</em>, and <em>z</em><sub>s</sub> (Narita et al. 2019). We regressed those depths against the effective wavelength of each band. The report publishes no per-band uncertainty, so we use the scatter about the mean only to describe the scale of the variation and the fitted slope. It does not support a calibrated achromaticity test.

Second, an eclipsing stellar companion on the target would move the host by a velocity semi-amplitude far larger than a planetary one. We computed the circular, edge-on semi-amplitude that companions of 0.1, 0.3, and 0.6 M<sub>☉</sub> would produce at the adopted period, assuming a {{HOST_MASS}} M<sub>☉</sub> host, and compared them with the span of the three public reconnaissance velocities. This is a scale comparison, not an orbit fit: the observed quantity is the range of three recon velocities, not a fitted amplitude, and the host mass is assumed rather than measured.

### 4.1. Ground light curve

The {{GROUND_USED}} accepted ground measurements have an unbinned robust scatter of {{GROUND_SCATTER}} ppt. The fixed historical-window fit gives {{GROUND_DEPTH}} ± {{GROUND_DEPTH_ERROR}} ppt, or {{GROUND_DEPTH_SNR}}σ when positive depth denotes dimming. The negative value is therefore not a transit-like event. The adopted current-catalog prediction was already {{CURRENT_GROUND_OFFSET_HOURS}} h before the first accepted ground exposure, so the ground sequence did not observe the current predicted event.

Adding a {{INJECTION_DEPTH}} ppt same-window box recovered an incremental depth of {{INJECTION_RECOVERED}} ppt. Including the measured negative control at that location, the total recovered dimming has signal-to-noise {{INJECTION_TOTAL_SNR}}. The injection demonstrates sensitivity to a catalog-scale dimming at the fixed historical window; it does not establish sensitivity to every transit shape or blend scenario.

The whole-sequence search finds no compelling event at any time it can test. Requiring a well-sampled event with baseline on both sides restricts the catalog-duration midpoints to {{SEARCH_RANGE_LOW}}–{{SEARCH_RANGE_HIGH}} h after the first exposure, because the sequence is only about twice the transit duration. Within that range the largest fitted dimming is {{SEARCH_BEST_DEPTH}} ± {{SEARCH_BEST_ERROR}} ppt, a formal single-midpoint S/N of {{SEARCH_BEST_SIGMA}}. The median formal 3σ WLS upper bounds are {{SEARCH_FORMAL_LIMIT_CATALOG}} ppt for the {{SEARCH_DURATION_CATALOG}} h catalog duration and {{SEARCH_FORMAL_LIMIT_SPOC}} ppt for the {{SEARCH_DURATION_SPOC}} h SPOC duration, against published depths of {{CATALOG_DEPTH}} and {{SPOC_DEPTH}} ppt. These bounds omit temporal covariance and are therefore not interpreted as global exclusions. ## 5. Discussion

In Section 5.1 we interpret the 2022 null observation. In Section 5.2 we discuss the precision and scope of the timing solution. In Section 5.3 we place the candidate in the context of the transiting-planet population. In Section 5.4 we work through the standard false-positive scenarios. In Section 5.5 we set out what the candidate status and the unresolved blends do and do not permit us to conclude. In Section 5.6 we define the literature and novelty boundary of this work.

### 5.1. Interpretation of the 2022 null

The ground light curve alone answers a limited question: no transit-like dimming is measured inside the exact window the 2022 schedule told the observers to cover. The same-window injection shows that a catalog-scale box would have been recovered in that portion of the data. The updated TESS ephemeris then resolves the apparent conflict by placing the event predicted by the adopted linear ephemeris outside the sequence.

The schedule reconstruction provides a specific historical explanation. The three marker cells are jointly reproduced to better than one minute by a public 2021 prediction propagated with the superseded period. That residual is close to the floor the source data can express: the schedule records its ingress and egress only to the nearest minute, so agreement much better than the {{MAX_MARKER_OFFSET}} s achieved here could not be demonstrated from these cells even if the underlying formula were recovered exactly. A coincidental match of ingress, midpoint, and egress is less plausible than a single midpoint coincidence because it also preserves the event duration. Nevertheless, we cannot inspect the missing workbook formulas or revision history. We therefore describe the result as a strongly supported archival reconstruction, not proof that a particular spreadsheet formula caused the error.

The null observation should not be used as evidence that the TESS signal disappeared. It is an off-transit observation under the adopted ephemeris. Conversely, explaining the schedule does not turn the ground data into a transit detection.

### 5.2. Timing precision and model scope

The long, four-sector baseline supplies most of the period leverage. Sectors 14 and 81 are separated by more than five years, while the two-minute SPOC sampling in the later sectors improves individual midpoint precision. The consistency of the period under conservative sector aggregation, stricter selection, event deletion, sector deletion, QLP-only processing, and the external MuSCAT2 control indicates that no tested event or threshold alone determines the headline value.

The formal uncertainty remains conditional on the approximate trapezoid, the event-level timing errors, and the single linear ephemeris. The four-sector jackknife is larger but is based on only four clusters. Reporting both quantities distinguishes a fit uncertainty from a coarse sensitivity scale. The positive ΔBIC for the quadratic model means that these timings do not justify an added curvature parameter; it does not prove that the physical period is exactly constant.

### 5.3. The candidate in context

The published parameters place TOI-3505.01 among short-period giant-planet candidates. The TOI catalog lists a planet radius of {{CATALOG_RADIUS}} R<sub>⊕</sub> and an insolation of {{CATALOG_INSOLATION}} S<sub>⊕</sub>, and the host is a late-F dwarf with <em>T</em><sub>eff</sub> = {{TARGET_TEFF}} K and {{TARGET_RADIUS}} R<sub>☉</sub>. On a {{PERIOD_SHORT}} d orbit, the catalog radius is larger than Neptune's but below Jupiter's. We quote these as catalog values: this work does not fit a radius, and the standing dilution limits described in Section 5.5 are the reason.

The geometry is the complicating factor. The SPOC multi-sector solution gives an impact parameter of {{SPOC_IMPACT}}, which is close to grazing. Near-grazing transits couple depth, duration, and impact parameter tightly, so a depth measured without a documented dilution model does not translate cleanly into a radius. This is why we replace the box with an exposure-integrated trapezoid for timing (Section 3.2) but stop short of a physical fit, and it is the main reason a radius from this work would be less reliable than the catalog value we cite.

### 5.4. False-positive scenarios

Taking the standard scenarios in turn, each is constrained by at least one line of evidence, and none is excluded outright.

A nearby eclipsing binary is disfavoured by the screen in Section 3.5: of {{NEARBY_BRIGHT}} catalogued neighbours bright enough to matter, none that could be measured shows an eclipse-shaped event of the required depth, and the two that the screen cannot clear on depth alone are monotonic trends across the night rather than events. Public nearby-star checks accompanying the KeplerCam, ULMT, and MuSCAT2 observations report the same, and the SPOC difference-image centroid sits {{CENTROID_SIGMA}}σ from the catalogued position in both sectors, so the source of the dimming coincides with the target at TESS resolution. What this leaves open is the population too faint for a decisive limit, and anything inside the target aperture.

A blended eclipsing binary is weakly disfavoured by the lack of an apparent monotonic trend in the four reported MuSCAT2 depths. Without per-band uncertainties, however, those points do not establish calibrated achromaticity. A blend whose colour resembles the target could also evade this screen, so the result is qualitative and does not exclude the scenario.

An eclipsing stellar companion on the target itself is the most strongly disfavoured. It would move the host by at least {{STELLAR_MIN_K}} km s<sup>−1</sup>, and the public velocities span {{RV_SPAN}} km s<sup>−1</sup>. The SPOC odd/even, secondary-eclipse, and eclipsing-binary diagnostics agree. The caveat is that a definitive mass awaits the multi-order analysis the public notes leave open.

None of this reaches the scenario that matters most. The {{COMPANION_SEPARATION}} arcsec companion is unresolved in every dataset used here, and the tests above constrain the star the spectrograph and the imagers resolved, not the pair. That is why the sections below claim no validation and no radius.

### 5.5. Candidate status and unresolved blends

The recurring signal and TESS-scale centroid agreement are compatible with a transit on the target system. The public reconnaissance velocities bear on this from a different instrument and a different dataset: the observing note interprets a velocity offset in phase with the photometric ephemeris as consistent with an approximately {{RV_MASS}}-Jupiter-mass companion. That report is not a closed result, since its author recorded scatter and left the target on hold, and we do not re-derive it. Even taken at face value it does not determine which component of the unresolved system is dimming, because a {{COMPANION_SEPARATION}} arcsec companion is inseparable in the TESS images, in the 25 pixel ground aperture, and in a fiber-fed reconnaissance spectrum. The conditional nearby-star screen also leaves {{NEARBY_OVERLAP}} apertures overlapping the target and is not a formal nearby-eclipsing-binary clearance.

These limitations are why we do not apply the scenario dilution correction, infer a planet-to-star radius ratio, calculate a planet radius, or report a false-positive probability. The live public status at the analysis date remains PC. Statistical validation would require a documented combination of resolved imaging, spectroscopy or radial velocities as appropriate, fully characterized light curves, and a formal false-positive analysis. As Section 2.4 records, resolved imaging and spectroscopic epochs for this target already exist in the public archive; what is missing is not the observations but a joint analysis of them, which is beyond the scope of this timing and archival study.

### 5.6. Literature and novelty boundary

TOI-3505.01 appears in the population-level Ariel target list of Edwards & Tinetti (2022), and public ground follow-up predates this analysis. Exact searches for “TOI-3505.01,” “TOI 3505,” and TIC 390988385 found no dedicated paper or preprint in the literature snapshot, refreshed on 2026 August 29 across arXiv, OpenAlex, the NASA Exoplanet Archive bibliography, and general scholarly web results. The supported novelty is therefore the combination of all four public TESS sectors with the archival schedule reconstruction, not discovery or first follow-up. We additionally confirmed within the observing program that no parallel study of this target is in progress. Because a search can miss an unexpected identifier or unindexed venue, and because that confirmation cannot extend to unpublished work elsewhere, the phrase “To our knowledge” must remain, and the search should be repeated immediately before submission or preprint posting.

## 6. Conclusions and future work

We analyzed the GMU 0.8 m R-band sequence and all four public TESS sectors of TOI-3505.01. The ground data show no dimming in the confirmed-EDT 2022 schedule window: {{GROUND_DEPTH}} ± {{GROUND_DEPTH_ERROR}} ppt. The whole-sequence scan contains no formal 3σ event, but exact published-depth injections cross that threshold at {{SEARCH_RECOVERY_CATALOG}} and {{SEARCH_RECOVERY_SPOC}} of the admissible midpoints for the two tested durations; the data therefore do not support exclusion at every phase. The primary TESS-only fit gives <em>P</em> = {{PERIOD_SHORT}} ± {{PERIOD_ERROR_SHORT}} d from {{EVENTS}} events over {{BASELINE_YEARS}} yr. The larger {{SECTOR_JACKKNIFE_ERROR}} d delete-one-sector scale records the sensitivity to the four widely separated observing clusters, and the remaining tested controls do not motivate a different headline period. The event predicted by the adopted ephemeris was {{CURRENT_OFFSET_HOURS}} h before the schedule midpoint, while a superseded {{OLD_PERIOD}} d public ephemeris reproduces all three schedule markers within {{MAX_MARKER_OFFSET}} s. The simplest supported interpretation is that the 2022 observations followed a stale event window.

Future work should obtain a new, full transit using the updated prediction and an independently verified observatory clock, and should complete the multi-order velocity analysis that the public notes leave open and combine it with the resolved imaging already held in the archive. Those, together with a documented crowding treatment, are what a formal false-positive assessment requires. A joint, exposure-integrated, limb-darkened transit model should be attempted only after the dilution model is documented, and a longer sequence than the one analyzed here would be needed to search for events near the ends of a night. Recovering the original 2022 workbook, formula history, Transit Info file, and clock record would further strengthen the archival interpretation. Until those steps are complete, TOI-3505.01 remains a planet candidate and no dilution-corrected radius should be quoted.
